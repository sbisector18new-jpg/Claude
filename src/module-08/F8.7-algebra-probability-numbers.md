## 1. Session 1 — Quadratic Equations ★★★★★

### 1.1 The three ways to solve one

For **ax² + bx + c = 0** with a not zero:

| Method | When to use it |
|---|---|
| **Factorisation** | First choice. Split b into two numbers whose **product is ac** and whose **sum is b** |
| **The formula** | x = [−b ± root(b² − 4ac)] / 2a — when factors are not obvious |
| Completing the square | Rarely needed in an objective paper |

> **Worked.** x² − 5x + 6 = 0. Two numbers with product 6 and sum −5: **−2 and −3**. So
> (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0 and the roots are **2 and 3** — Q2.

### 1.2 The discriminant — the whole of "the nature of the roots"

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · D = b² − 4ac, AND THE FOUR CASES
The **discriminant D = b² − 4ac** decides the nature of the roots without your finding them.

| D | The roots are |
|---|---|
| **D > 0**, not a perfect square | **Real, distinct and irrational** |
| **D > 0** and a perfect square | **Real, distinct and rational** |
| **D = 0** | **Real and EQUAL** — a repeated root, −b/2a |
| **D < 0** | **Imaginary** (no real root) |

**Two consequences that are asked directly:**

1. **"For what value of k are the roots equal?"** means **set D = 0 and solve for k**. For
   x² − 6x + k = 0: 36 − 4k = 0, so **k = 9** — Q12
2. **Imaginary roots always occur in conjugate pairs.** So "one root real and one imaginary" is never a
   possible answer for an equation with real coefficients — it is offered as an option in Q11 and can be
   eliminated on sight

Note the vocabulary trap: **"real and equal" is a single case, D = 0.** Candidates who read "equal" as
"two identical answers, so not really two roots" get confused into choosing "imaginary". The equation
x² + 4x + 4 = 0 has the root −2 twice, and that is *real and equal*.
:::

:::

## 2. Session 2 — Sum and Product of Roots ★★★★★

### 2.1 The two relations

If α and β are the roots of **ax² + bx + c = 0**, then

> **α + β = −b/a** and **αβ = c/a**

That is the whole of it, and it is the highest-return pair of formulas in the Quant block.

> **Worked.** 3x² − 12x + 7 = 0. Sum = −(−12)/3 = **4**; product = 7/3 — Q3. Note that the roots
> themselves are irrational and you never needed them.
>
> **Worked.** 2x² + 5x − 8 = 0. Product = c/a = −8/2 = **−4** — Q4. The sign matters: c is negative, so
> the product is negative, meaning the roots have **opposite signs**.

### 2.2 Forming an equation from its roots

> **x² − (sum)x + (product) = 0**

Note the **minus** before the sum. For roots 3 and −5: sum = −2, product = −15, so the equation is
x² + 2x − 15 = 0 — Q13. The sign error here is the commonest slip in the topic, and both signs are
offered as options.

### 2.3 The symmetric functions — where the marks are

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · FOUR EXPRESSIONS YOU CAN EVALUATE WITHOUT FINDING THE ROOTS
Each of these is built from the sum and the product alone. Learn the four rearrangements:

| Wanted | Equals | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **α² + β²** | **(α + β)² − 2αβ** | Expand the square and subtract the cross terms |
| **1/α + 1/β** | **(α + β) / αβ** | Add the fractions over a common denominator |
| **α − β** | root[(α + β)² − 4αβ] | The square of the difference is D/a² |
| **α³ + β³** | (α + β)³ − 3αβ(α + β) | |

> **Worked.** α and β are the roots of x² − 6x + 8 = 0. Then α + β = 6 and αβ = 8, so
> **α² + β² = 36 − 16 = 20** — Q29. (The roots happen to be 2 and 4, and 4 + 16 = 20, but finding them
> was unnecessary.)
>
> **Worked.** For x² − px + q = 0, **1/α + 1/β = p/q** — Q21. One line, no roots.

**And the same algebra in disguise.** *If x + 1/x = 3, find x² + 1/x².* Square both sides:
x² + 2 + 1/x² = 9, so the answer is **7** — Q9. Likewise *a + b = 7, ab = 12* gives
a² + b² = 49 − 24 = **25** — Q19.

**Whenever a question gives you a sum and a product and asks for a sum of squares, do not solve for the
individual values.** Square the sum and subtract twice the product. It is one line, and it works even
when the individual values are irrational.
:::

## 3. Session 3 — Linear Equations and Identities ★★★★☆

### 3.1 Simultaneous equations

Two equations, two unknowns. **Substitution** is faster than elimination when one equation already gives a
variable cheaply.

> **Worked.** 2x + 3y = 12 and 3x − y = 7. The second gives y = 3x − 7. Substituting:
> 2x + 9x − 21 = 12, so 11x = 33 and **x = 3**, whence y = 2 — Q20.

**Always substitute your answer back into the equation you did not use.** In an objective paper you can
also test the options directly, which is often the fastest route of all when the options are small
integers.

### 3.2 The identities worth having by heart

| Identity | |
|---|---|
| (a + b)² | a² + 2ab + b² |
| (a − b)² | a² − 2ab + b² |
| **a² − b²** | **(a + b)(a − b)** |
| **a² + b²** | **(a + b)² − 2ab** |
| (a + b)³ | a³ + b³ + 3ab(a + b) |
| **a³ + b³** | **(a + b)(a² − ab + b²)** |
| **a³ − b³** | **(a − b)(a² + ab + b²)** |
| a³ + b³ + c³ − 3abc | (a + b + c)(a² + b² + c² − ab − bc − ca) |

::: know | KNOW · IF a + b + c = 0 THEN a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc
This follows from the last identity: when the first bracket is zero, the whole expression is zero, so
a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc.

It appears in question banks precisely because it looks like it needs work and does not. Whenever three
quantities sum to zero, this relation is the intended route.
:::

### 3.3 Word problems into equations

The translation is mechanical, and three phrases account for most of it:

- *"is"* becomes **=**
- *"of"* becomes **×**
- *"a number and its reciprocal"* becomes **x + 1/x**

> **Worked.** The sum of a number and its reciprocal is 26/5. Then x + 1/x = 26/5, so 5x² − 26x + 5 = 0,
> which factorises as (5x − 1)(x − 5) = 0, giving **x = 5** or 1/5 — Q22. Both are valid; the options
> decide which is wanted.

2. Write a² + b² in terms of (a + b) and ab
3. Factorise a³ − b³
4. If a + b + c = 0, what is a³ + b³ + c³?
:::

## 4. Session 4 — Probability ★★★★★

### 4.1 The definition and its consequences

> **P(E) = (number of favourable outcomes) ÷ (total number of equally likely outcomes)**

| Result | Statement |
|---|---|
| **Range** | 0 <= P(E) <= 1 · impossible event 0 · certain event 1 |
| **Complement** | **P(not E) = 1 − P(E)** — often far easier than E itself |
| **Addition** | P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B) · for **mutually exclusive** events the last term is 0 |
| **Multiplication** | For **independent** events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) |
| **Without replacement** | The second draw's denominator **falls by one** — the events are not independent |

### 4.2 The four sample spaces you must know cold

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · THE DENOMINATORS, AND THE PACK OF CARDS
Almost every probability error at this level is a **wrong denominator**. Memorise these four:

| Experiment | Total outcomes |
|---|---|
| One coin | 2 · **n coins: 2ⁿ** |
| **One die** | **6** · **two dice: 36** |
| One card from a pack | **52** |
| Two cards **without** replacement | 52 × 51 ordered, or **C(52,2) = 1,326** unordered |

**The pack of 52, which is examined more than anything else in the topic:**

- **4 suits** of 13 — spades and clubs **black**, hearts and diamonds **red**, so **26 of each colour**
- **4 cards of each rank**, so P(a king) = 4/52 = **1/13** — Q5
- **Face cards are the King, Queen and Jack: 3 per suit, so 12 in all** » P = 12/52 = **3/13** — Q16
- **Honours**, if the term is used, add the Ace: 4 per suit, 16 in all
- There is **no** "joker" in the 52

**Two dice — the sums.** The number of ways to make each sum is 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, **6**, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 for
sums 2 through 12. So P(sum 7) = 6/36 = 1/6, the most likely sum, and **P(sum 9) = 4/36 = 1/9** — Q14,
the four ways being (3,6), (4,5), (5,4) and (6,3).

**Note that (3,6) and (6,3) are different outcomes.** Treating them as one is the single commonest
mistake in two-dice questions, and it produces exactly the halved answer offered as a distractor.
:::

### 4.3 With and without replacement

> **Worked — without replacement.** Two cards are drawn from a pack, without replacement. P(both aces) =
> (4/52) × (3/51) = 12/2,652 = **1/221** — Q23. The second fraction has **3** aces out of **51** cards,
> because one ace has gone.
>
> **With replacement** it would be (4/52)² = 1/169 — which is option (a) in Q23, waiting for the candidate
> who did not notice.
>
> **Worked — by combinations.** A bag has 4 red and 6 black balls; two are drawn. P(both red) =
> C(4,2)/C(10,2) = 6/45 = **2/15** — Q24. The sequential route gives the same thing: (4/10) × (3/9) = 2/15.

**Either route works, and the combinations route is safer** when the question says "two are drawn together",
because it never tempts you into worrying about order.

### 4.4 When to use the complement

If a question asks for *"at least one"*, compute the probability of **none** and subtract. *At least one
head in three tosses* = 1 − P(no heads) = 1 − 1/8 = **7/8**. Counting "at least one" directly means
counting three cases; the complement means counting one.

## 5. Session 5 — Counting, As Far As Needed ★★★☆☆

### 5.1 The one distinction

| | Meaning | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| **Permutation** nPr | Arrangements — **order matters** | n! / (n − r)! |
| **Combination** nCr | Selections — **order does not matter** | **n! / [r!(n − r)!]** |

**A committee is a combination; a ranking is a permutation.** That single question — *does order matter?* —
decides every problem at this level.

### 5.2 Computing nCr quickly

**nCr = (n × (n−1) × … r terms) ÷ r!** Cancel before multiplying.

- C(4,2) = (4 × 3)/2 = **6**
- C(10,2) = (10 × 9)/2 = **45**
- C(5,2) = (5 × 4)/2 = **10**
- C(52,2) = (52 × 51)/2 = **1,326**

**Useful facts:** **nC0 = nCn = 1**, **nC1 = n**, and **nCr = nC(n−r)** — so C(10,8) is C(10,2) = 45, which
saves a great deal of arithmetic.

### 5.3 The "and" rule

When a selection has independent parts, **multiply**.

> **Worked.** From 5 men and 4 women, a committee of 3 with exactly 2 men and 1 woman:
> C(5,2) × C(4,1) = 10 × 4 = **40** — Q25.

The word **"exactly"** is what makes this one product. Had it said *"at least 2 men"*, you would add the
2-men case and the 3-men case: 40 + C(5,3) = 40 + 10 = 50. **Read whether the question says *exactly*,
*at least* or *at most* — that word, not the arithmetic, is what is being tested.**

::: check | CHECK YOURSELF · SESSION 5

3. From 6 men and 5 women, how many committees of 3 with exactly 1 woman?
4. What changes if the question says "at least one woman"?

## 6. Session 6 — The Number System ★★★★☆

### 6.1 HCF and LCM

| Idea | Statement |
|---|---|
| **HCF** | The largest number dividing all of them — take the **lowest** power of each common prime |
| **LCM** | The smallest number divisible by all of them — take the **highest** power of every prime |
| **The product rule** | **HCF × LCM = the product of the two numbers** (two numbers only) |
| Fractions | HCF of fractions = HCF of numerators / LCM of denominators |

> **Worked.** The HCF of two numbers is 12 and their LCM is 180; one number is 36. Then the other is
> (12 × 180)/36 = **60** — Q28. The product rule turns this into one division.

### 6.2 The two remainder patterns that are always asked

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · "THE SAME REMAINDER" MEANS TAKE THE HCF OF THE DIFFERENCES
These two are the standard number-system questions, and each has a one-line method.

**Pattern 1 — the greatest number that divides several numbers leaving the SAME remainder.**
The remainder cancels when you subtract, so take the **HCF of the differences**.

> 43, 91 and 183: the differences are 48, 92 and 140, whose HCF is **4** — Q17. (Check: each of 43, 91 and
> 183 leaves remainder 3 on division by 4.)

**Pattern 2 — the least number that, divided by several numbers, leaves the same remainder r.**
Take the **LCM and add r**.

> Divided by 6, 7 and 8 leaving 3: LCM = 168, so the number is **171** — Q18.

**And the variant:** if the remainders are each **less than the divisor by the same amount** — say
remainders 5, 6 and 7 on division by 6, 7 and 8 — then the number is **LCM minus that amount**, here
168 − 1 = 167.

**A last one worth knowing.** If a number leaves remainder 7 on division by 15, then on division by **5**
it leaves 7 mod 5 = **2**, because 15 is a multiple of 5 — Q30. Where one divisor divides the other,
reduce the remainder.
:::

### 6.3 Divisibility rules

| By | Test |
|---|---|
| 2, 5, 10 | Last digit |
| **3, 9** | **Sum of the digits** divisible by 3 or 9 |
| 4 | Last **two** digits |
| 8 | Last **three** digits |
| **6** | Divisible by **2 and 3** |
| **11** | The difference of the alternating digit sums is 0 or a multiple of 11 |
| 7 | Double the last digit, subtract from the rest, repeat |

### 6.4 Indices, and remainders of powers

**The laws:** aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ · aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ · (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ · a⁰ = 1 · a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ ·
a^(1/n) is the nth root.

> **Worked.** (2⁵ × 2³) ÷ 2⁴ = 2⁸⁻⁴ = 2⁴ = **16** — Q10.

**Remainders of large powers** are found by spotting a cycle:

> **Worked.** The remainder when 2²⁰ is divided by 7. Since 2³ = 8 leaves remainder **1** on division by 7,
> powers of 2 cycle every three. Write 20 = 3 × 6 + 2, so 2²⁰ = (2³)⁶ × 2², which leaves 1⁶ × 4 = **4** —
> Q27.

**The method in general:** find the smallest power of the base that leaves remainder 1, and use it to strip
the exponent down. This is the only form in which large powers are asked at this level.

::: check | CHECK YOURSELF · SESSION 6

4. Remainder when 3¹⁰⁰ is divided by 5? *(Hint: 3⁴ = 81 leaves 1)*
5. Give the divisibility tests for 8, 9 and 11
:::

## 8. Answers and Explanations

### 8.1 Level 1

| Q | Ans | Explanation |
|---|:-:|---|
| 1 | c | b² − 4ac = 49 − 24 = **25**. Being a perfect square, the roots are rational — here 3 and ½ |
| 2 | b | Product 6, sum −5, so the factors are −2 and −3 and the roots are **2 and 3**. Note the roots have the **opposite** signs to the factors, which is what (a) is testing |
| 3 | a | −b/a = 12/3 = **4**. (c) is the product and (d) is −b without the division |
| 4 | d | c/a = −8/2 = **−4**. The negative product tells you the roots have opposite signs; (c) is the same magnitude with the sign dropped |
| 5 | c | 4 kings in 52 cards = **1/13**. (d) is the number of kings over the number of ranks — a wrong denominator |
| 6 | b | Three even faces of six = **1/2** |
| 7 | a | 24 = 2³ × 3, 36 = 2² × 3², so the HCF takes the **lowest** powers: 2² × 3 = **12**. (c) is the LCM |
| 8 | d | Highest powers: 2² × 3 × 5 = **60**. (c) is a common multiple but not the least — the standard distractor |
| 9 | b | Square both sides: x² + 2 + 1/x² = 9, so x² + 1/x² = **7**. (a) is the square before subtracting the 2 |
| 10 | c | 2⁵⁺³⁻⁴ = 2⁴ = **16** |

### 8.2 Level 2

| Q | Ans | Explanation |
|---|:-:|---|
| 11 | a | D = 16 − 16 = 0, so the roots are **real and equal**, both −2. (d) is impossible for real coefficients — imaginary roots come in conjugate pairs, per §1.2 |

## 9. One-Minute Revision

::: check | ONE-MINUTE REVISION · CHAPTER F8.7
**WEIGHT** **quadratic roots and probability both appeared in 2023** · target 45–75 seconds

**QUADRATIC** ax² + bx + c = 0 · x = [−b ± root(b² − 4ac)]/2a · factorise by splitting b into two numbers
with **product ac and sum b**

**DISCRIMINANT D = b² − 4ac** · **D > 0 real distinct** (perfect square » rational) · **D = 0 real and
EQUAL** · **D < 0 imaginary** · "equal roots" means **set D = 0 and solve for k** · imaginary roots come in
**conjugate pairs**, so "one real one imaginary" is never possible

**SUM AND PRODUCT** **α + β = −b/a · αβ = c/a** · form the equation as **x² − (sum)x + product = 0** —
note the **minus**

**SYMMETRIC FUNCTIONS** **α² + β² = (α+β)² − 2αβ** · **1/α + 1/β = (α+β)/αβ** · α − β = root[(α+β)² − 4αβ]
· α³ + β³ = (α+β)³ − 3αβ(α+β) · **the same trick: x + 1/x = 3 » x² + 1/x² = 7**

**if a + b + c = 0 then a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc**

**PROBABILITY** favourable ÷ total · 0 to 1 · **P(not E) = 1 − P(E)**, use it for **"at least one"** ·
mutually exclusive » add · independent » multiply · **without replacement the denominator falls by one**
