## 1. Session 1 — Percentage ★★★★★

### 1.1 The three questions percentage asks

| Question | Method |
|---|---|
| **What is x% of N?** | Convert x% to a fraction, multiply |
| **A is what per cent of B?** | (A/B) × 100 |
| **A is what per cent more or less than B?** | (difference ÷ **B**) × 100 — the **base is what you compare to** |

### 1.2 The fraction method

The percentages that appear in examinations are not random. They are the ones with clean fractional
equivalents, because the examiner also wants the arithmetic to close.

| Percentage | Fraction | Percentage | Fraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50% | 1/2 | 12½% | 1/8 |
| 33⅓% | 1/3 | 11⅑% | 1/9 |
| 25% | 1/4 | 9¹/₁₁% | 1/11 |
| 20% | 1/5 | 8⅓% | 1/12 |
| 16⅔% | 1/6 | 6¼% | 1/16 |
| 14²/₇% | 1/7 | 5% | 1/20 |
| **37½%** | **3/8** | **62½%** | **5/8** |
| **66⅔%** | **2/3** | **83⅓%** | **5/6** |

> **Worked.** A basic wage of ₹18,000 rises by 16⅔%. New wage = 18,000 × **7/6** = **₹21,000**. Compare
> the decimal route: 18,000 × 0.1667 = 3,000.06, then add. The fraction route is faster and exact.

### 1.3 The four standard shapes

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · THE FOUR PERCENTAGE RESULTS WORTH KNOWING BY HEART
**1. A is x% more than B, so B is less than A by x/(100 + x) × 100.**
If A is 25% more than B, then B is 25/125 = **20% less** than A. The two percentages are **never the
same**, because the base changes. This is the commonest percentage trap in existence — Q1

**2. Increase by x% then decrease by x% is always a net LOSS of x²/100 per cent.**
Up 20% then down 20% leaves 0.8 × 1.2 = 0.96, a **4% fall** — never "no change" — Q8

**3. If the price rises by x%, consumption must fall by x/(100 + x) × 100 to keep expenditure the same.**
A 25% rise needs a **20% cut**, not 25% — Q28

**4. Two successive discounts of a% and b% equal a single discount of (a + b − ab/100) per cent.**
20% and 10% together give 28%, not 30% — Q13

All four are the same underlying point: **percentages do not add, because the base moves.** Multiply the
factors instead — 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72 — and read the answer off the product.
:::

### 1.4 The base — where most marks are lost

*A is 20% of B* and *B is 20% more than A* involve completely different bases. Before computing, name the
base in one word: **per cent of what?** If the question says *"increased by"*, the base is the original
value; if it says *"of"*, the base is the noun that follows.

::: check | CHECK YOURSELF · SESSION 1
1. Give fractions for 16⅔%, 12½%, 37½%, 66⅔%, 8⅓%
2. If A is 25% more than B, by what per cent is B less than A?
3. A sum rises 10% then falls 10%. Net change?
4. Price rises 20%; by how much must consumption fall to hold expenditure constant?
5. Single equivalent of successive discounts of 25% and 20%?
:::

## 2. Session 2 — Ratio and Proportion ★★★★★

### 2.1 The basics, stated precisely

A ratio compares quantities **of the same kind** and has no units. *a : b* is the fraction *a/b*, and it
is unchanged if both terms are multiplied by the same number — which is why every ratio problem is solved
by finding the value of **one part**.

| Idea | Statement |
|---|---|
| **Proportion** | a : b = c : d, so **ad = bc** — the product of the extremes equals that of the means |
| **Compounded ratio** | Combine a:b and b:c by making the common term equal: a:b:c |
| **Duplicate / sub-duplicate** | a²:b² · and the square-root ratio |
| **Dividing a sum** | In a : b : c, each share is *its part ÷ total parts* of the sum |
| **Direct proportion** | More men, more work: multiply |
| **Inverse proportion** | More men, **fewer days**: divide |

### 2.2 The one-part method

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · FIND WHAT ONE PART IS WORTH, THEN ANSWER
Almost every ratio question in this examination reduces to a single line of arithmetic once you find the
value of one part.

> **Worked — a sum divided.** ₹6,300 is divided in the ratio 2 : 3 : 4. Total parts = 9, so **one part =
> 700**, and the shares are 1,400, 2,100 and 2,800.
>
> **Worked — a difference given.** A and B share a sum in the ratio 5 : 3, and A gets ₹800 more than B.
> The **difference is 2 parts**, so one part = 400, and the total, being 8 parts, is **₹3,200**. Note that
> the question never told you the total — the difference did.
>
> **Worked — combining ratios.** a : b = 2 : 3 and b : c = 4 : 5. Make *b* common: multiply the first by 4
> and the second by 3 to get a : b : c = **8 : 12 : 15**, so a : c = **8 : 15**.

**The discipline:** write down what one part is worth **before** you look at the options. Candidates who
skip that step answer with a part instead of a total, which is exactly the wrong option offered.
:::

### 2.3 Adding to a ratio — the shape that recurs

When a quantity is **added to one component only**, the other component is unchanged, and that unchanged
quantity is your anchor.

> **Worked.** 45 litres of a mixture is milk and water in the ratio 4 : 1, so milk = 36 L and water = 9 L.
> How much water makes it 3 : 2? **The milk stays 36.** In a 3 : 2 mixture with 36 of milk, water must be
> 24. So **add 15 litres.** Q31 is this question.

::: trap | TRAP · THE UNCHANGED QUANTITY IS THE ONE TO FIX
When water is added, milk is constant. When one salary is raised, the other is constant. Set up the new
ratio **in terms of the constant**, not in terms of a new variable.

Candidates who write "let x litres be added" and solve (36)/(9 + x) = 3/2 arrive at the same answer, but
slower and with more chances to slip. Anchoring on the constant makes it mental arithmetic.
:::

::: check | CHECK YOURSELF · SESSION 2
1. Divide ₹8,400 in the ratio 3 : 4 : 5
2. A : B = 3 : 4 and B : C = 6 : 7. Find A : C
3. Two shares are in the ratio 7 : 4 and differ by ₹900. What is the total?
4. In 60 litres of a 5 : 1 milk–water mixture, how much water makes it 5 : 2?
:::

## 3. Session 3 — Averages ★★★★★

### 3.1 The only formula, and its two rearrangements

> **Average = total ÷ number of items.** Therefore **total = average × number**, and that second form is
> the one you actually use.

**Almost every average question is solved by converting averages into totals, doing the arithmetic on
totals, and converting back.** The word "average" in a question is an instruction to multiply.

| Shape | Method |
|---|---|
| **A member is replaced** | Change in total = **number × change in average** |
| **A member is added** | New total − old total = the new member's value |
| **Overlapping groups** | Add the group totals and subtract the whole, to isolate the overlap |
| **A wrong reading is corrected** | Change in average = (error ÷ number of items) |

### 3.2 The replacement rule

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · CHANGE IN TOTAL = NUMBER × CHANGE IN AVERAGE
This one line answers the 2023 *"mean of weights"* family in a single step.

> **Worked.** The average weight of 8 men is 62 kg. A man weighing 55 kg is replaced and the average
> becomes 63 kg. The average rose by 1 kg over 8 men, so the **total rose by 8 kg**, so the new man weighs
> 55 + 8 = **63 kg**.

No equations, no unknowns. The same rule handles a member being **added**: if 30 members average 32 years
and adding one raises the average to 33, the newcomer's age is 31 × 33 − 30 × 32 = **63** — Q16.

**And corrections:** if one observation of 96 was read as 69, the total is short by 27, so over 25
observations the average is short by 27/25 = **1.08** — Q30.
:::

### 3.3 Overlapping groups — the subtraction trick

> **Worked.** A, B and C average 45 kg. A and B average 40; B and C average 43. Find B.
>
> (A + B) + (B + C) = 80 + 86 = 166, and A + B + C = 135. Subtracting, **B = 31 kg**.

The pattern generalises: where two overlapping pairs and the whole are given, **the sum of the pairs minus
the whole leaves the overlap**. Q25 and Q10 are both this shape.

::: check | CHECK YOURSELF · SESSION 3
1. State the replacement rule
2. Average of 10 numbers is 45; one number 27 is replaced by 47. New average?
3. The average of 11 results is 50, of the first six 49, of the last six 52. Find the sixth
4. Average of 20 observations was 45; an observation of 61 was read as 16. Correct average?
:::

## 4. Session 4 — Mixtures and Alligation ★★★★☆

### 4.1 The alligation rule

For **two** ingredients mixed to a known mean value:

> **(quantity of cheaper) : (quantity of dearer) = (price of dearer − mean) : (mean − price of cheaper)**

The differences are taken **crosswise**, and the result is a ratio of **quantities**.

> **Worked.** Milk at ₹40 a litre is mixed with water, which is free, so that the mixture costs ₹32 a
> litre. Ratio of milk to water = (32 − 0) : (40 − 32) = 32 : 8 = **4 : 1**.
>
> **Worked, with two mixtures.** Vessel I has milk and water in the ratio 5 : 2, vessel II in the ratio
> 3 : 4. In what ratio must they be mixed to get equal milk and water? Work in **milk fractions**: 5/7 and
> 3/7, target 1/2. Ratio = (1/2 − 3/7) : (5/7 − 1/2) = 1/14 : 3/14 = **1 : 3**.

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · ALWAYS ALLIGATE ON THE SAME COMPONENT
The single mechanical error in alligation is mixing the components: taking the milk fraction of one vessel
and the water fraction of the other.

**Choose one component — milk, or alcohol, or the cheaper item — and express all three values as that
component's share.** Then apply the crosswise rule.

And note what the answer is: a ratio of the **quantities of the two mixtures**, not of their components.
Q27 offers the reversed ratio 3 : 1 as an option for exactly this reason.
:::

### 4.2 Repeated replacement

When *y* units are drawn from *x* units of pure liquid and replaced by water, and this is done *n* times:

> **Liquid remaining = x × [(x − y)/x]ⁿ**

> **Worked.** 20 litres of milk; 4 litres drawn off and replaced by water, twice. Milk left = 20 ×
> (16/20)² = 20 × 0.64 = **12.8 litres** — Q19.

**Note the trap:** the formula gives the **liquid remaining**, not the amount removed, and it applies only
when the volume is restored each time.

::: check | CHECK YOURSELF · SESSION 4
1. State the alligation rule
2. In what ratio must rice at ₹30 and ₹45 a kg be mixed to sell at ₹35?
3. From 40 litres of milk, 8 litres are removed and replaced by water, twice. Milk left?
4. Why must alligation be done on one component only?
:::

## 5. Session 5 — Problems on Ages ★★★★☆

### 5.1 The method

Ages are ratio problems with a time shift. Three rules make them mechanical:

1. **Let the present ages be the ratio with an x** — 7x and 3x, not two separate unknowns. One variable,
   one equation
2. **Add or subtract the shift from EVERY age.** Ten years hence, both are ten years older
3. **Form the equation from the second ratio** and cross-multiply

> **Worked — the 2023 ratio-and-ages shape.** The ages of a father and son are in the ratio 7 : 3, and ten
> years hence the ratio will be 2 : 1. Then (7x + 10)/(3x + 10) = 2/1, so 7x + 10 = 6x + 20, giving x = 10
> and the father's present age **70**. Check: 70 and 30 become 80 and 40, which is 2 : 1 — Q26.

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · CHECK BY SUBSTITUTION, IT COSTS FIVE SECONDS
Age problems are the easiest place in the Quant paper to make a sign error, and the **only** place where
verification is completely free: put your answer back into the words of the question.

If the father is 70 and the son 30, then in ten years they are 80 and 40 — a ratio of 2 : 1, as required.
Five seconds, and it converts a probable mark into a certain one.

**The examiner's standard trap is to offer the value of x, or the son's age, when the father's was asked.**
In the worked example, x = 10 and the son is 30; both are wrong answers to the question asked. Before you
mark, re-read **whose** age was wanted.
:::

### 5.2 The two other standard forms

| Form | Example | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| **A multiple now, another later** | A father is 3 times his son's age; in 12 years, twice | 3s + 12 = 2(s + 12), so s = 12 |
| **A multiple now, another earlier** | 3 times now; 15 years ago, 9 times | 3s − 15 = 9(s − 15), so s = 20 |

Both are one linear equation. Note in the second that the larger multiple belongs to the **earlier** time —
ratios of ages always move **towards 1 : 1** as people get older, which is a useful sanity check on any
answer.

::: check | CHECK YOURSELF · SESSION 5
1. Ages are 5 : 2; in 6 years, 2 : 1. Find both
2. A man is 4 times his son; in 20 years, twice. Find both
3. Why must a ratio of ages move towards 1 : 1 over time?
4. What are the two wrong answers an age question usually offers?
:::

## 6. Session 6 — Time and Work ★★★★☆

### 6.1 The LCM method — the technique to learn

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · TAKE THE TOTAL WORK AS THE LCM OF THE DAYS
Do not add fractions. Take the **total work as the LCM of the given numbers of days**, and every rate
becomes a whole number of units per day.

> **Worked.** A does a job in 12 days, B in 18. LCM = **36 units** of work. A does 3 units a day, B does 2.
> Together 5 a day, so they finish in **36/5 = 7.2 days** — Q7.
>
> **Worked, one leaves.** A in 20 days, B in 30. LCM = **60 units**; A does 3 a day, B does 2, together 5.
> In 6 days they complete 30 units — exactly half. The remaining 30 units at A's 3 a day take **10 days** —
> Q29.
>
> **Worked, efficiency given.** A is twice as efficient as B and together they take 12 days. Let B do 1
> unit a day and A 2, so together 3 a day and the job is 36 units. **B alone: 36 days** — Q21.

Every fraction has disappeared. This is the single highest-return technique in the chapter, and it also
prevents the commonest error in the topic — **adding days instead of adding rates**.
:::

### 6.2 The rules

| Situation | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Together** | Add the **rates**, never the days |
| **A alone, from together** | B's rate = (together's rate) − (A's rate) |
| **Efficiency** | Twice as efficient means **half the time**; rates are in the ratio of efficiencies |
| **Wages** | Wages divide in the ratio of **work done**, not of days worked |
| **Men and days** | M₁D₁/W₁ = M₂D₂/W₂ — more men, fewer days |
| **Pipes** | An emptying pipe is a **negative** rate |

### 6.3 Wages — the shape that catches people

> **Worked.** A and B contract a job for ₹2,400. A alone would take 20 days, B 30. With C's help they
> finish in 10 days. What is C's share?
>
> Take 60 units. A does 3 a day, B 2, so A + B = 5. To finish in 10 days the team must do 6 a day, so **C
> does 1 unit a day**. Over 10 days: A 30 units, B 20, **C 10** — a sixth of the work, so **₹400** — Q35.

The trap is to divide the money by three, or in the ratio of days. **Wages follow work done.**

::: check | CHECK YOURSELF · SESSION 6
1. Why take the LCM as the total work?
2. A in 15 days, B in 10. How long together?
3. A and B together in 8 days, A alone in 12. B alone?
4. In what ratio do wages divide?
:::

## 7. Session 7 — The Lean Tail ★★★☆☆

Three topics that are **not** in the 2023 evidence but are one step from what is. They get one session
between them, and that is a deliberate judgement about yield, not an oversight: percentage is the parent
of all three, so if you have §1 you have most of this already.

### 7.1 Average speed

> **Average speed = total distance ÷ total time.** Never the average of the speeds.

For **equal distances** at speeds u and v, average speed = **2uv/(u + v)** — the harmonic mean.

> **Worked.** Half the distance at 40 km/h, half at 60. Average = 2 × 40 × 60/100 = **48 km/h**, not 50 —
> Q23. It is below 50 because more time is spent at the slower speed.

For **equal times**, the average *is* the arithmetic mean. **Equal distances give the harmonic mean;
equal times give the arithmetic mean** — and the question always tells you which by saying *half the
distance* or *half the time*. F9.1 §2.2's conversion applies here: **km/h to m/s is × 5/18**.

### 7.2 Profit and loss

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Profit %** | (SP − CP)/**CP** × 100 — always on **cost** |
| **SP from a gain** | SP = CP × (100 + gain%)/100 |
| **CP from SP** | CP = SP × 100/(100 + gain%) |
| **Discount** | On the **marked price**, not on cost |

::: trap | TRAP · EQUAL SELLING PRICES, EQUAL PERCENTAGES, ONE GAIN ONE LOSS » ALWAYS A LOSS
Two articles sold at the **same price**, one at a gain of x% and one at a loss of x%, always produce an
**overall loss of x²/100 per cent**.

> **Worked.** Two articles at ₹1,200 each, one at +20%, one at −20%. Cost prices are 1,200 × 100/120 =
> 1,000 and 1,200 × 100/80 = 1,500, so total cost 2,500 against total sale 2,400: a loss of ₹100 on
> ₹2,500 = **4%** — which is 20²/100 — Q33.

The reason is the same as §1.3's second result: **the two percentages have different bases.** The loss is
taken on the larger cost price, so it outweighs the gain. "No profit no loss" is the wrong option waiting
for you.
:::

### 7.3 Simple and compound interest

> **SI = PRT/100.** **CI:** amount = P(1 + R/100)ᵗ, and CI = amount − P.

| Result | Value |
|---|---|
| **Difference between CI and SI, 2 years** | **P(R/100)²** |
| Difference, 3 years | P(R/100)² × (300 + R)/100 |
| **Compounded half-yearly** | Rate halves, time doubles |
| **Money doubles under SI** | When RT = 100 |

> **Worked.** ₹10,000 for 2 years at 10%. SI = 2,000. CI = 10,000 × 1.21 − 10,000 = 2,100. Difference
> **₹100**, which is 10,000 × (10/100)² — Q32.

The two-year difference is worth memorising, because it converts a two-line computation into a one-step
multiplication, and it is the only form of the question that is commonly set.

1. **The moving base.** Q1, Q8, Q13, Q28, Q33. **Five of thirty-five questions** are the same insight —
   percentages cannot be added or reversed, because the base changes. Multiply the factors instead.
   Learning this one idea properly is worth more than any other page in the chapter

   totals, do the arithmetic, divide back
4. **LCM instead of fractions.** Q7, Q15, Q21, Q29, Q35. Five questions, no fractions needed in any of

5. **The intuitive answer offered first.** Q8(a) "no change", Q13(b) "30%", Q23(a) "50", Q28(b) "25%",

## 10. One-Minute Revision

::: check | ONE-MINUTE REVISION · CHAPTER F8.5
**WEIGHT** the largest slice of the 15-question Quant block — **ratio and ages, mixtures, mean of weights**
all appeared in 2023 · **target 45–60 seconds**

**FRACTIONS** 16⅔% = 1/6 · 12½% = 1/8 · 37½% = 3/8 · 62½% = 5/8 · 66⅔% = 2/3 · 83⅓% = 5/6 · 8⅓% = 1/12 ·
14²/₇% = 1/7 · **an increase of 16⅔% means × 7/6**

**PERCENTAGE — THE FOUR RESULTS** A is x% more than B » B is **x/(100 + x)** less than A (25% more » 20%
less) · up x% then down x% » **loss of x²/100** · price up x% » cut consumption by **x/(100 + x)** ·
successive discounts a and b » **a + b − ab/100**. **PERCENTAGES DO NOT ADD — MULTIPLY THE FACTORS**

**RATIO** find what **one part** is worth first · a difference of shares is a difference of **parts** ·
combine ratios by making the common term equal · when adding to a mixture, **anchor on the unchanged
component**

**AVERAGE** total = average × number · **change in total = number × change in average** · overlapping
pairs: **sum of pairs − whole = the overlap** · a misread observation shifts the average by **error ÷ n**

**ALLIGATION** cheaper : dearer = (dearer − mean) : (mean − cheaper), **crosswise** · always alligate on
**one component** · repeated replacement: **x[(x − y)/x]ⁿ** remains

**AGES** use one variable **7x and 3x** · add the shift to **every** age · **substitute back to check** ·
ratios move **towards 1 : 1** with time · the trap is x, or the wrong person's age

**TIME AND WORK** **total work = LCM of the days** · add **rates**, never days · twice as efficient = half
the time · **wages divide in the ratio of work DONE** · emptying pipe = negative rate · M₁D₁ = M₂D₂

**SPEED** average speed = total distance ÷ total time · equal **distances** » harmonic mean **2uv/(u + v)**
· equal **times** » arithmetic mean · km/h to m/s **× 5/18**

**PROFIT** always on **cost** · discount on **marked price** · same SP with +x% and −x% » **always a loss of
x²/100**

**INTEREST** SI = PRT/100 · CI difference over **2 years = P(R/100)²** · half-yearly: rate halves, time
doubles · SI doubles money when RT = 100
