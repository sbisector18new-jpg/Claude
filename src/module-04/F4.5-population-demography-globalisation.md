## 1. Session 1 — Demographic Concepts ★★★★★

### 1.1 The rates, and what each is measured per

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · THE DENOMINATOR OF EVERY DEMOGRAPHIC RATE
Almost every question in this session is answered by knowing **what a rate is measured per**. Learn the
right-hand column.

| Measure | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Crude Birth Rate (CBR)** | Live births **per 1,000 population** per year |
| **Crude Death Rate (CDR)** | Deaths **per 1,000 population** per year |
| **Natural growth rate** | **CBR − CDR** — excludes migration |
| **Total Fertility Rate (TFR)** | Average number of children **per woman** over her reproductive life |
| **Infant Mortality Rate (IMR)** | Deaths under **one year** of age **per 1,000 LIVE BIRTHS** |
| **Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR)** | Maternal deaths **per 1,00,000 LIVE BIRTHS** |
| **Under-five mortality** | Deaths before age five per 1,000 live births |
| **Sex ratio** | **Females per 1,000 males** (the Indian convention) |
| Child sex ratio | Females per 1,000 males in the **0–6** age group |
| **Density** | Persons **per square kilometre** |
| **Dependency ratio** | Population **outside** the working age group (0–14 and 60+) **÷ working-age population** |
| **Replacement level fertility** | A TFR of about **2.1** |

**The three that are most often got wrong:**

1. **IMR is per 1,000 live births; MMR is per 1,00,000 live births.** Different denominators, and both are
   offered for each other. MMR uses the larger base because maternal deaths are far rarer
2. **The sex ratio in India is females per 1,000 males** — the reverse of the convention in several other
   countries. A ratio *below* 1,000 therefore means fewer women than men
3. **Replacement level is 2.1, not 2.0.** The extra tenth allows for girls who do not survive to
   reproductive age, which is why the figure exceeds two
:::

### 1.2 The demographic transition

The theory describes the path from high birth and death rates to low ones as an economy develops.

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Population |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 High stationary** | **High** | **High** | Nearly **stable** — growth is slow because deaths offset births |
| **2 Early expanding** | High | **Falling** | **Growing fast** — the widest gap opens here |
| **3 Late expanding** | **Falling** | Low | Still growing, but decelerating |
| **4 Low stationary** | **Low** | **Low** | Nearly **stable** again |
| 5 Declining | Very low | Low | **Shrinking** |

**The trap in stage 1 and stage 4:** both have **near-stable populations**, but for opposite reasons — high
births offset by high deaths in stage 1, low births matched by low deaths in stage 4. A question describing
"both rates high and population growing slowly" is stage 1; "both rates low" is stage 4.

**Population growth is fastest in stage 2**, when death rates have fallen but birth rates have not yet
followed. That lag is the whole mechanism of population explosion.

### 1.3 Where India now stands

::: know | KNOW · INDIA'S FERTILITY HAS FALLEN BELOW REPLACEMENT  `[AS AT 16 AUG 2026]` · RE-VERIFY
| Source | TFR |
|---|---|
| **Sample Registration System, 2024 report** | **1.9** |
| **NFHS-6 (2023-24)** | **2.0** |
| **Replacement level** | **2.1** |

**On either figure, India is below replacement level.** Only about **six States** now record a TFR above
replacement, and India is among the majority of countries and territories worldwide with fertility below
replacement.

Sources: [Times of India on the SRS 2024 figure of 1.9](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/india/only-6-states-now-have-fertility-rate-above-replacement-level/articleshow/131285680.cms) ·
[Data for India on the TFR](https://www.dataforindia.com/fertility/) ·
[PRS Vital Stats on replacement level](https://prsindia.org/policy/vital-stats/national-family-health-survey-5)

**Note the two different figures**, exactly as with GDP growth in F4.1 §3.1: the **SRS** and the **NFHS** are
different instruments and give slightly different readings. Both are below 2.1, so the *conclusion* is
robust even though the *number* depends on the source. If an option set contains both, read the stem for
which survey it names.

**The apparent paradox worth understanding:** India's **population is still rising** while fertility is below
replacement. There is no contradiction — **population momentum** means a young age structure keeps producing
more births than deaths for decades after fertility falls, because a large cohort of women is still passing
through the reproductive ages. *Content rephrased from the sources for compliance with licensing
restrictions.*

## 2. Session 2 — The Census ★★★★☆

### 2.1 The machinery

| Fact | |
|---|---|
| **First census** | **1872**, under **Lord Mayo** — **non-synchronous** |
| **First synchronous census** | **1881**, under **Lord Ripon** |
| Periodicity | **Decennial**, uninterrupted from 1881 until 2011 |
| **Legal basis** | The **Census Act, 1948** |
| **Conducting authority** | The **Registrar General and Census Commissioner of India**, under the **Ministry of Home Affairs** |
| Constitutional entry | **Union List, Entry 69** |

**The 1872 / 1881 pair is the standard question.** 1872 was the first census; **1881 was the first
synchronous one**, and the unbroken decennial series is counted from 1881.

### 2.2 The 2011 profile — still the latest completed census

The 2021 census was postponed, so **Census 2011 remains the most recent completed enumeration**, and its
figures are still the ones examined.

| Indicator | 2011 |
|---|---|
| **Population** | about **1,210.9 million** (121 crore) |
| Decadal growth | about **17.7%** |
| **Density** | **382** persons per sq km |
| **Sex ratio** | **943** females per 1,000 males |
| **Child sex ratio (0–6)** | **919** — a historic low |
| **Literacy** | about **74%** |
| Most populous State | **Uttar Pradesh** |
| **Most densely populated State** | **Bihar** |
| **Highest sex ratio (State)** | **Kerala**, about 1,084 |
| **Lowest sex ratio (State)** | **Haryana**, about 879 |

**The one to note is the child sex ratio of 919**, lower than the overall 943 — meaning the imbalance was
**worse among children than among adults**, which is the finding that makes it a policy question rather than
a historical one.

## 3. Session 3 — The Demographic Dividend ★★★★☆

### 3.1 The concept

> The **demographic dividend** is the growth potential that arises when the **share of the working-age
> population (15–59 or 15–64) is high** relative to dependants — a **temporary window**, opened by falling
> fertility and closed by ageing.

**Three points that follow, and all three are examinable:**

1. **It is a window, not a condition.** It opens when fertility falls (fewer children to support) and closes
   as the large working cohort ages into dependency. India's window is generally put as lasting into the
   **2050s**
2. **It is potential, not automatic.** The dividend is realised only if the working-age population is
   **educated, healthy and employed**. A large young population without jobs is a liability, not a dividend —
   which connects directly to F4.4 §2's low participation rates
3. **The dependency ratio is the operative statistic**, and it **falls** when the dividend opens

**India's median age is now about 28 to 29 years**, against the low 40s in most of Europe and East Asia — the
basis for the claim that India will supply a large share of the world's incremental workforce.

### 3.2 Population policy

| Item | |
|---|---|
| First official family planning programme | **1952** — India was the **first country in the world** to adopt one |
| **National Population Policy** | **2000** |
| NPP's stated goal | Achieving **replacement level fertility** — the target date of 2010 was not met, though the goal has since been reached, per §1.3 |
| National Commission on Population | 2000, chaired by the **Prime Minister** |
| Mission Parivar Vikas | Focused effort in high-fertility districts |

**Two facts worth holding:** **India was the first country to adopt a national family planning programme, in
1952**, and the **National Population Policy is of 2000**. Both are commonly asked and easily confused with
the emergency-era coercive episode of 1976, which was a programme excess rather than a policy.

::: know | KNOW · WHY THE DIVIDEND MATTERS TO THE EPFO SPECIFICALLY
A provident fund and a pension scheme are **demographically leveraged institutions**. While the working-age
share is rising, contributions grow faster than claims and the corpus builds. When the dividend closes and
the dependency ratio rises, **claims grow faster than contributions**.

That is the actuarial background to everything in Module 3: the Employees' Pension Scheme is a defined
benefit obligation whose cost depends on how long members live after retirement, which is precisely what
F6.4's AS 15 distinction between defined contribution and defined benefit plans is about.

**For the interview:** India's demographic window is the reason the EPFO's finances look comfortable now,
and the reason coverage expansion — bringing gig and platform workers in, per the Code on Social Security,
2020 — is more urgent than the current surplus suggests.
:::

## 4. Session 4 — Globalisation and the External Sector ★★★★☆

### 4.1 The 1991 reforms

| Fact | |
|---|---|
| **The trigger** | A **balance of payments crisis** — reserves down to a few weeks of imports |
| **The programme** | **Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation (LPG)** — the New Economic Policy |
| Finance Minister | **Manmohan Singh**, under Prime Minister **P V Narasimha Rao** |
| Measures | Abolition of industrial **licensing** for most industries, **devaluation**, tariff reduction, opening to FDI, **disinvestment**, reduction of the public sector's reserved areas |
| **FERA replaced** | **FERA, 1973 replaced by FEMA, 1999** — a shift from *regulation* to *management*, and from criminal to civil penalties |

### 4.2 The balance of payments

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · WHICH ACCOUNT EACH ITEM GOES IN
| Account | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Current account** | **Merchandise trade** (visibles) **+ invisibles** — services, **remittances**, and investment income |
| **Capital account** | **FDI, FPI, external borrowing, banking capital**, and changes in **reserves** |

**The item most often misplaced is remittances.** Personal transfers from Indians working abroad are
**invisibles in the CURRENT account** — not the capital account, and **not** net factor income from abroad,
which is a national-accounting concept covering *factor* income rather than transfers. F4.1 §1.1 makes the
same point from the other side, and the two together are why India's current account benefits from
remittances while its GNP does not.

**FDI against FPI** — the distinction is **control**:

- **FDI** brings **management participation**, is long-term, and is harder to reverse
- **FPI** is investment in **securities without control**, and is **volatile** — "hot money"

**Convertibility:** the rupee is **fully convertible on the CURRENT account** (since 1994, under Article
VIII of the IMF's Articles) but **NOT fully convertible on the capital account**. The **Tarapore
Committees** laid out a road map for capital account convertibility that has not been fully implemented.
That asymmetry is the single most examined fact about the rupee.
:::

### 4.3 The institutions

| Institution | |
|---|---|
| **IMF and World Bank** | Created at the **Bretton Woods Conference, 1944**; both headquartered in **Washington DC** |
| IMF's function | Short-term balance of payments support; its unit of account is the **Special Drawing Right (SDR)** |
| World Bank Group | **IBRD, IDA, IFC, MIGA, ICSID** — IDA lends on concessional terms |
| **GATT** | **1947**, a provisional agreement, not an organisation |
| **WTO** | Established **1 January 1995**, by the **Marrakesh Agreement** concluding the **Uruguay Round**. **Headquarters Geneva**. India is a **founder member** |
| WTO decision-making | By **consensus**; it has a binding **Dispute Settlement** mechanism, which GATT lacked |

**The GATT-to-WTO transition is the standard question:** **GATT 1947 was an agreement; the WTO from 1
January 1995 is an organisation** with legal personality and enforceable dispute settlement.
