## 1. Session 1 — Units, Scalars and Vectors ★★★★☆

### 1.1 The seven SI base units

| Quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Length | metre | m |
| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Time | second | s |
| Electric current | ampere | A |
| Thermodynamic temperature | kelvin | K |
| Amount of substance | mole | mol |
| Luminous intensity | candela | cd |
^ Seven base units — the count itself is asked

### 1.2 Derived units worth knowing by name

| Unit | Quantity | In base units |
|---|---|---|
| **newton** (N) | Force | kg m s⁻² |
| **joule** (J) | Work, energy | N m |
| **watt** (W) | Power | J s⁻¹ |
| **pascal** (Pa) | Pressure | N m⁻² |
| **hertz** (Hz) | Frequency | s⁻¹ |
| **coulomb** (C) | Electric charge | A s |
| **dioptre** (D) | Power of a lens | m⁻¹ |

### 1.3 Scalars and vectors

**Scalars** have magnitude only: mass, **speed**, distance, **work**, **energy**, power, time,
temperature, density, pressure, electric charge.

**Vectors** have magnitude and direction: displacement, **velocity**, acceleration, **force**,
**momentum**, **weight**, impulse, torque.

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · THE THREE PAIRS THE EXAM USES
1. **Work and energy are SCALARS**, even though work is defined using force, which is a vector. The dot
   product of two vectors is a scalar. This is the single most common wrong option in the topic
2. **Speed is scalar, velocity is vector.** Likewise **distance** scalar, **displacement** vector
3. **Mass is a scalar and is constant. Weight is a vector and varies** with location, because W = mg and
   g varies

Pressure is also a scalar, which surprises people, because although force has direction, pressure at a
point acts equally in all directions.
:::

## 2. Session 2 — Kinematics ★★★★★

### 2.1 The equations of motion

For **uniform (constant) acceleration**:

| Equation | Does not contain |
|---|---|
| **v = u + at** | displacement s |
| **s = ut + ½at²** | final velocity v |
| **v² = u² + 2as** | **time t** |
| **s = [(u + v)/2] × t** | acceleration a |
^ Choose the equation by what the question does not give you

Where u is initial velocity, v final velocity, a acceleration, t time and s displacement.

For **free fall**, a = g ≈ 9.8 m s⁻² downwards (take 10 where the question permits). Thrown upwards, use
a = −g.

### 2.2 Graphs

| Graph | Slope gives | Area under gives |
|---|---|---|
| **Distance–time** | Speed | — |
| **Velocity–time** | **Acceleration** | **Displacement** |
| Acceleration–time | — | Change in velocity |

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · THE UNIT CONVERSION THAT DECIDES NUMERICALS
**To convert km/h into m/s, multiply by 5/18. To go the other way, multiply by 18/5.**

So **72 km/h = 20 m/s**, and **36 km/h = 10 m/s**. Nearly every kinematics numerical in this examination
is set in km/h and must be solved in SI, and the 2023 paper's question on the **uniform acceleration of
a train** is exactly this shape.

Do the conversion first, before you choose an equation. The commonest error is not the physics — it is
solving correctly in the wrong units.
:::

> Worked, in the 2023 style: a train starting from rest reaches 72 km/h in 20 s under uniform
> acceleration.
> 72 km/h = 72 × 5/18 = 20 m s⁻¹. Then a = (v − u)/t = (20 − 0)/20 = **1 m s⁻²**

## 3. Session 3 — Laws of Motion and Collisions ★★★★★

### 3.1 Newton's three laws

| Law | Statement | What it gives us |
|---|---|---|
| **First** | A body continues at rest or in uniform motion unless acted on by an external force | Defines **inertia**, and defines force qualitatively |
| **Second** | Force equals the rate of change of momentum; **F = ma** when mass is constant | Defines force **quantitatively**, and gives the newton |
| **Third** | To every action there is an equal and opposite reaction | Pairs of forces, and conservation of momentum |

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · TWO POINTS ABOUT THE THIRD AND SECOND LAWS
1. **Action and reaction act on DIFFERENT bodies**, and therefore **never cancel each other**. A
   statement that they cancel, or that they act on the same body, is wrong. This is the single most
   examinable subtlety in the laws of motion
2. **The general form of the second law is F = dp/dt**, force equals the rate of change of momentum.
   **F = ma is the special case where mass is constant.** So an option saying the second law applies
   only when mass is constant is wrong — it is *F = ma* that carries that restriction, not the law

Three kinds of inertia are sometimes asked by name: inertia of **rest**, of **motion**, and of
**direction**.
:::

**Momentum** p = mv, a vector, unit kg m s⁻¹. **Impulse** = F × t = **change in momentum**, unit N s.

### 3.2 Collisions — the 2023 pattern

| | **Elastic** | **Inelastic** | **Perfectly inelastic** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Momentum conserved | **Yes** | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Kinetic energy conserved | **Yes** | **No** | **No — loss is maximum** |
| After impact | Bodies separate | Bodies separate | Bodies **move together** |
| Coefficient of restitution e | **e = 1** | 0 < e < 1 | **e = 0** |

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · MOMENTUM IS CONSERVED IN EVERY COLLISION
**Linear momentum is conserved in all collisions. Kinetic energy is conserved only in an elastic
collision.**

That one sentence answers most collision questions, and the 2023 paper's inelastic-collision item is
built on it. The trap is an option asserting that momentum is not conserved in an inelastic collision —
it always is, because conservation of momentum follows from Newton's third law and holds regardless of
whether energy is lost to heat, sound or deformation.

**Coefficient of restitution** e = (relative velocity of separation) / (relative velocity of approach).
e = 1 perfectly elastic, e = 0 perfectly inelastic.
:::

> Worked, perfectly inelastic: 2 kg at 5 m s⁻¹ strikes a stationary 3 kg body and they move together.
> Momentum before = 2 × 5 = 10 kg m s⁻¹. After, (2 + 3) v = 10, so **v = 2 m s⁻¹**.
> Check the energy: before = ½ × 2 × 25 = 25 J; after = ½ × 5 × 4 = 10 J. **15 J lost** — which is
> exactly why it is inelastic.

### 3.3 Circular motion

**Centripetal force = mv²/r**, directed **towards** the centre — it is the real force that keeps a body
on a circular path.

**Centrifugal force** is a **pseudo (fictitious) force**, apparent only in a rotating frame, directed
**away** from the centre. Calling it a real force is a standard wrong option.

## 4. Session 4 — Work, Energy and Power ★★★★☆

### 4.1 Work

> **W = F s cos θ**, where θ is the angle between force and displacement. Unit: **joule**. A **scalar**.

| θ | cos θ | Work |
|---|---|---|
| 0° | 1 | Maximum, positive |
| 90° | 0 | **Zero** |
| 180° | −1 | Negative |

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · WHEN WORK IS ZERO
Work is **zero when the force is perpendicular to the displacement**, because cos 90° = 0.

Three standard illustrations, each of which has appeared in one form or another:

- **Carrying a bucket while walking on level ground** — the force is vertical, the displacement
  horizontal, so the work done against gravity is **zero**
- A **satellite in a circular orbit** — the gravitational force is towards the centre, the displacement
  tangential, so gravity does **no work**
- A **coolie carrying a load on a level platform** — the same situation as the bucket

Note the counter-intuitive framing: the man is certainly getting tired, but the *physics* definition of
work is zero. The exam relies on you confusing effort with work.
:::

### 4.2 Energy and power

| Quantity | Formula | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Kinetic energy | **½mv²** | joule |
| Potential energy | **mgh** | joule |
| Power | **W/t** | **watt** |

- **Work–energy theorem:** the work done by the net force equals the **change in kinetic energy**
- **1 horsepower ≈ 746 W**
- **The commercial unit of electrical energy is the kilowatt-hour: 1 kWh = 3,600,000 J**, that is
  3.6 million joules

::: check | CHECK YOURSELF · SESSION 4
1. Give the work formula. When is work zero, and give two examples
2. Is work a scalar or a vector?
3. State the work–energy theorem
4. How many joules in a kilowatt-hour? How many watts in a horsepower?
:::

## 5. Session 5 — Gravitation and Fluids ★★★★☆

### 5.1 Gravitation

> **F = G m₁ m₂ / r²** and **g = GM/R²**

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **g varies** | Decreases with **altitude** and with **depth**; zero at the centre of the Earth |
| **g is maximum at the poles**, minimum at the equator | Because the Earth bulges at the equator and rotates |
| **Escape velocity** from Earth | About **11.2 km s⁻¹** |
| **Orbital velocity** near the surface | About **7.9 km s⁻¹** |
| Relationship | **Escape velocity = √2 × orbital velocity** |

::: must-master | MUST MASTER · WEIGHTLESSNESS IS NOT THE ABSENCE OF GRAVITY
An astronaut in an orbiting satellite feels weightless because the astronaut **and** the satellite are
both in **free fall** — they accelerate towards the Earth at the same rate, so there is no contact force
between them.

Gravity is very much present; indeed it is what keeps the satellite in orbit. The option "there is no
gravity in space" is wrong, and it is the intuitive answer.

The state is properly called **apparent weightlessness**, and the same thing happens in a lift whose
cable is cut.
:::

### 5.2 Fluids — four principles

| Principle | Statement | Application |
|---|---|---|
| **Pascal's law** | Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted **equally in all directions** | **Hydraulic press, hydraulic brakes**, hydraulic lift |
| **Archimedes' principle** | The upthrust equals the **weight of the fluid displaced** | Flotation, ships, submarines, hydrometer |
| **Bernoulli's principle** | Where the velocity of a fluid is **high**, its pressure is **low** | **Aerofoil lift**, atomiser, spin of a ball, Venturi meter |
| **Law of flotation** | A floating body displaces its **own weight** of fluid | Ships, icebergs |

> Pressure = Force / Area, unit **pascal**. Atmospheric pressure ≈ 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa, measured by a
> barometer.

::: trap | COMMON UPSC TRAP · WHICH PRINCIPLE EXPLAINS WHICH DEVICE
The three fluid principles are constantly swapped in matching questions. Fix them by the device:

- **Hydraulic brakes and presses » Pascal** — transmission of pressure
- **Ships, submarines and hydrometers » Archimedes** — upthrust
- **Aeroplane lift, atomiser, swinging cricket ball » Bernoulli** — velocity and pressure

If the device *multiplies force*, it is Pascal. If it *floats or sinks*, it is Archimedes. If something
*moves through or past a fluid*, it is Bernoulli.
:::

::: check | CHECK YOURSELF · SESSION 5
1. Where is g maximum, and why?
2. Give escape and orbital velocities, and the relationship between them
3. Why does an astronaut feel weightless? What is the wrong explanation?
4. Match: hydraulic brake, ship, aerofoil — to their principles
5. State the law of flotation
:::
