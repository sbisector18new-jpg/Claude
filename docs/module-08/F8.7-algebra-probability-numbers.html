HIGH YIELD · TWO NAMED 2023 ITEMS LIVE IN THIS CHAPTER

Blueprint §4.4 records quadratic roots and probability among the fifteen 2023 Quant questions. Both are in this chapter, and both are the kind of question where a single remembered relation converts a two-minute problem into a ten-second one.

The quadratic item is the clearest example in the whole Quant block of a question you should never solve. If a question asks for the sum of the roots, or their product, or α² + β², or 1/α + 1/β, you do not need the roots. −b/a and c/a answer all four. §2 is the most profitable page in the chapter.

Probability at this level is counting, not theory. Every item reduces to favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes, and the only real skill is counting the denominator correctly — which is why §4.2's four-line table of standard sample spaces is worth more than any formula.

1. Session 1 — Quadratic Equations

1.1 The three ways to solve one

For ax² + bx + c = 0 with a not zero:

MethodWhen to use it
FactorisationFirst choice. Split b into two numbers whose product is ac and whose sum is b
The formulax = [−b ± root(b² − 4ac)] / 2a — when factors are not obvious
Completing the squareRarely needed in an objective paper

Worked. x² − 5x + 6 = 0. Two numbers with product 6 and sum −5: −2 and −3. So (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0 and the roots are 2 and 3 — Q2.

1.2 The discriminant — the whole of "the nature of the roots"

MUST MASTER · D = b² − 4ac, AND THE FOUR CASES

The discriminant D = b² − 4ac decides the nature of the roots without your finding them.

DThe roots are
D > 0, not a perfect squareReal, distinct and irrational
D > 0 and a perfect squareReal, distinct and rational
D = 0Real and EQUAL — a repeated root, −b/2a
D < 0Imaginary (no real root)

Two consequences that are asked directly:

  1. "For what value of k are the roots equal?" means set D = 0 and solve for k. For x² − 6x + k = 0: 36 − 4k = 0, so k = 9 — Q12
  2. Imaginary roots always occur in conjugate pairs. So "one root real and one imaginary" is never a possible answer for an equation with real coefficients — it is offered as an option in Q11 and can be eliminated on sight

Note the vocabulary trap: "real and equal" is a single case, D = 0. Candidates who read "equal" as "two identical answers, so not really two roots" get confused into choosing "imaginary". The equation x² + 4x + 4 = 0 has the root −2 twice, and that is real and equal.

  2. State the four cases of the discriminant

  4. Why can an equation with real coefficients never have exactly one imaginary root?

2. Session 2 — Sum and Product of Roots

2.1 The two relations

If α and β are the roots of ax² + bx + c = 0, then

α + β = −b/a and αβ = c/a

That is the whole of it, and it is the highest-return pair of formulas in the Quant block.

Worked. 3x² − 12x + 7 = 0. Sum = −(−12)/3 = 4; product = 7/3 — Q3. Note that the roots themselves are irrational and you never needed them.

Worked. 2x² + 5x − 8 = 0. Product = c/a = −8/2 = −4 — Q4. The sign matters: c is negative, so the product is negative, meaning the roots have opposite signs.

2.2 Forming an equation from its roots

x² − (sum)x + (product) = 0

Note the minus before the sum. For roots 3 and −5: sum = −2, product = −15, so the equation is x² + 2x − 15 = 0 — Q13. The sign error here is the commonest slip in the topic, and both signs are offered as options.

2.3 The symmetric functions — where the marks are

MUST MASTER · FOUR EXPRESSIONS YOU CAN EVALUATE WITHOUT FINDING THE ROOTS

Each of these is built from the sum and the product alone. Learn the four rearrangements:

WantedEqualsWhy
α² + β²(α + β)² − 2αβExpand the square and subtract the cross terms
1/α + 1/β(α + β) / αβAdd the fractions over a common denominator
α − βroot[(α + β)² − 4αβ]The square of the difference is D/a²
α³ + β³(α + β)³ − 3αβ(α + β)

Worked. α and β are the roots of x² − 6x + 8 = 0. Then α + β = 6 and αβ = 8, so α² + β² = 36 − 16 = 20 — Q29. (The roots happen to be 2 and 4, and 4 + 16 = 20, but finding them was unnecessary.)

Worked. For x² − px + q = 0, 1/α + 1/β = p/q — Q21. One line, no roots.

And the same algebra in disguise. If x + 1/x = 3, find x² + 1/x². Square both sides: x² + 2 + 1/x² = 9, so the answer is 7 — Q9. Likewise a + b = 7, ab = 12 gives a² + b² = 49 − 24 = 25 — Q19.

Whenever a question gives you a sum and a product and asks for a sum of squares, do not solve for the individual values. Square the sum and subtract twice the product. It is one line, and it works even when the individual values are irrational.

3. Session 3 — Linear Equations and Identities

3.1 Simultaneous equations

Two equations, two unknowns. Substitution is faster than elimination when one equation already gives a variable cheaply.

Worked. 2x + 3y = 12 and 3x − y = 7. The second gives y = 3x − 7. Substituting: 2x + 9x − 21 = 12, so 11x = 33 and x = 3, whence y = 2 — Q20.

Always substitute your answer back into the equation you did not use. In an objective paper you can also test the options directly, which is often the fastest route of all when the options are small integers.

3.2 The identities worth having by heart

Identity
(a + b)²a² + 2ab + b²
(a − b)²a² − 2ab + b²
a² − b²(a + b)(a − b)
a² + b²(a + b)² − 2ab
(a + b)³a³ + b³ + 3ab(a + b)
a³ + b³(a + b)(a² − ab + b²)
a³ − b³(a − b)(a² + ab + b²)
a³ + b³ + c³ − 3abc(a + b + c)(a² + b² + c² − ab − bc − ca)

KNOW · IF a + b + c = 0 THEN a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc

This follows from the last identity: when the first bracket is zero, the whole expression is zero, so a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc.

It appears in question banks precisely because it looks like it needs work and does not. Whenever three quantities sum to zero, this relation is the intended route.

3.3 Word problems into equations

The translation is mechanical, and three phrases account for most of it:

  • "is" becomes =
  • "of" becomes ×
  • "a number and its reciprocal" becomes x + 1/x

Worked. The sum of a number and its reciprocal is 26/5. Then x + 1/x = 26/5, so 5x² − 26x + 5 = 0, which factorises as (5x − 1)(x − 5) = 0, giving x = 5 or 1/5 — Q22. Both are valid; the options decide which is wanted.

  3. Factorise a³ − b³
  4. If a + b + c = 0, what is a³ + b³ + c³?

4. Session 4 — Probability

4.1 The definition and its consequences

P(E) = (number of favourable outcomes) ÷ (total number of equally likely outcomes)

ResultStatement
Range0 <= P(E) <= 1 · impossible event 0 · certain event 1
ComplementP(not E) = 1 − P(E) — often far easier than E itself
AdditionP(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B) · for mutually exclusive events the last term is 0
MultiplicationFor independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
Without replacementThe second draw's denominator falls by one — the events are not independent

4.2 The four sample spaces you must know cold

MUST MASTER · THE DENOMINATORS, AND THE PACK OF CARDS

Almost every probability error at this level is a wrong denominator. Memorise these four:

ExperimentTotal outcomes
One coin2 · n coins: 2ⁿ
One die6 · two dice: 36
One card from a pack52
Two cards without replacement52 × 51 ordered, or C(52,2) = 1,326 unordered

The pack of 52, which is examined more than anything else in the topic:

  • 4 suits of 13 — spades and clubs black, hearts and diamonds red, so 26 of each colour
  • 4 cards of each rank, so P(a king) = 4/52 = 1/13 — Q5
  • Face cards are the King, Queen and Jack: 3 per suit, so 12 in all » P = 12/52 = 3/13 — Q16
  • Honours, if the term is used, add the Ace: 4 per suit, 16 in all
  • There is no "joker" in the 52

Two dice — the sums. The number of ways to make each sum is 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 for sums 2 through 12. So P(sum 7) = 6/36 = 1/6, the most likely sum, and P(sum 9) = 4/36 = 1/9 — Q14, the four ways being (3,6), (4,5), (5,4) and (6,3).

Note that (3,6) and (6,3) are different outcomes. Treating them as one is the single commonest mistake in two-dice questions, and it produces exactly the halved answer offered as a distractor.

4.3 With and without replacement

Worked — without replacement. Two cards are drawn from a pack, without replacement. P(both aces) = (4/52) × (3/51) = 12/2,652 = 1/221 — Q23. The second fraction has 3 aces out of 51 cards, because one ace has gone.

With replacement it would be (4/52)² = 1/169 — which is option (a) in Q23, waiting for the candidate who did not notice.

Worked — by combinations. A bag has 4 red and 6 black balls; two are drawn. P(both red) = C(4,2)/C(10,2) = 6/45 = 2/15 — Q24. The sequential route gives the same thing: (4/10) × (3/9) = 2/15.

Either route works, and the combinations route is safer when the question says "two are drawn together", because it never tempts you into worrying about order.

4.4 When to use the complement

If a question asks for "at least one", compute the probability of none and subtract. At least one head in three tosses = 1 − P(no heads) = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8. Counting "at least one" directly means counting three cases; the complement means counting one.

5. Session 5 — Counting, As Far As Needed

5.1 The one distinction

MeaningFormula
Permutation nPrArrangements — order mattersn! / (n − r)!
Combination nCrSelections — order does not mattern! / [r!(n − r)!]

A committee is a combination; a ranking is a permutation. That single question — does order matter? — decides every problem at this level.

5.2 Computing nCr quickly

nCr = (n × (n−1) × … r terms) ÷ r! Cancel before multiplying.

  • C(4,2) = (4 × 3)/2 = 6
  • C(10,2) = (10 × 9)/2 = 45
  • C(5,2) = (5 × 4)/2 = 10
  • C(52,2) = (52 × 51)/2 = 1,326

Useful facts: nC0 = nCn = 1, nC1 = n, and nCr = nC(n−r) — so C(10,8) is C(10,2) = 45, which saves a great deal of arithmetic.

5.3 The "and" rule

When a selection has independent parts, multiply.

Worked. From 5 men and 4 women, a committee of 3 with exactly 2 men and 1 woman: C(5,2) × C(4,1) = 10 × 4 = 40 — Q25.

The word "exactly" is what makes this one product. Had it said "at least 2 men", you would add the 2-men case and the 3-men case: 40 + C(5,3) = 40 + 10 = 50. Read whether the question says exactly, at least or at most — that word, not the arithmetic, is what is being tested.

CHECK YOURSELF · SESSION 5

  3. From 6 men and 5 women, how many committees of 3 with exactly 1 woman?
  4. What changes if the question says "at least one woman"?

6. Session 6 — The Number System

6.1 HCF and LCM

IdeaStatement
HCFThe largest number dividing all of them — take the lowest power of each common prime
LCMThe smallest number divisible by all of them — take the highest power of every prime
The product ruleHCF × LCM = the product of the two numbers (two numbers only)
FractionsHCF of fractions = HCF of numerators / LCM of denominators

Worked. The HCF of two numbers is 12 and their LCM is 180; one number is 36. Then the other is (12 × 180)/36 = 60 — Q28. The product rule turns this into one division.

6.2 The two remainder patterns that are always asked

MUST MASTER · "THE SAME REMAINDER" MEANS TAKE THE HCF OF THE DIFFERENCES

These two are the standard number-system questions, and each has a one-line method.

Pattern 1 — the greatest number that divides several numbers leaving the SAME remainder. The remainder cancels when you subtract, so take the HCF of the differences.

43, 91 and 183: the differences are 48, 92 and 140, whose HCF is 4 — Q17. (Check: each of 43, 91 and 183 leaves remainder 3 on division by 4.)

Pattern 2 — the least number that, divided by several numbers, leaves the same remainder r. Take the LCM and add r.

Divided by 6, 7 and 8 leaving 3: LCM = 168, so the number is 171 — Q18.

And the variant: if the remainders are each less than the divisor by the same amount — say remainders 5, 6 and 7 on division by 6, 7 and 8 — then the number is LCM minus that amount, here 168 − 1 = 167.

A last one worth knowing. If a number leaves remainder 7 on division by 15, then on division by 5 it leaves 7 mod 5 = 2, because 15 is a multiple of 5 — Q30. Where one divisor divides the other, reduce the remainder.

6.3 Divisibility rules

ByTest
2, 5, 10Last digit
3, 9Sum of the digits divisible by 3 or 9
4Last two digits
8Last three digits
6Divisible by 2 and 3
11The difference of the alternating digit sums is 0 or a multiple of 11
7Double the last digit, subtract from the rest, repeat

6.4 Indices, and remainders of powers

The laws: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ · aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ · (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ · a⁰ = 1 · a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ · a^(1/n) is the nth root.

Worked. (2⁵ × 2³) ÷ 2⁴ = 2⁸⁻⁴ = 2⁴ = 16 — Q10.

Remainders of large powers are found by spotting a cycle:

Worked. The remainder when 2²⁰ is divided by 7. Since 2³ = 8 leaves remainder 1 on division by 7, powers of 2 cycle every three. Write 20 = 3 × 6 + 2, so 2²⁰ = (2³)⁶ × 2², which leaves 1⁶ × 4 = 4 — Q27.

The method in general: find the smallest power of the base that leaves remainder 1, and use it to strip the exponent down. This is the only form in which large powers are asked at this level.

30dThe number is 15k + 7. Since 15 is a multiple of 5, only the 7 matters, and 7 leaves 2 on division by 5. (a) is the trap of carrying the remainder across unchanged

THINK LIKE UPSC · THE FIVE SHAPES IN THIS BANK

  1. The question that does not need the roots. Q3, Q4, Q9, Q19, Q21, Q29 — six of thirty — all answered from −b/a and c/a alone. If you solved any of them by finding the roots, you spent three times the necessary effort. This is the highest-value habit in the chapter
  2. The intermediate value offered as an answer. Q9(a) is the square before subtracting 2; Q12(a) is root(k); Q29(a) and (b) are (α+β)² and 2αβ. In each case the wrong option is a real step in the correct working, which is why it looks familiar. Finish the calculation before you look at the options
  3. With or without replacement. Q23 and Q24 both offer the other version as a distractor. The phrase is the question; underline it
  4. The wrong denominator. Q5(d), Q15(c), Q16(b), Q14(b). Probability errors at this level are almost never in the numerator. Count the sample space first, and write it down
  5. The sign. Q2(a), Q4(c), Q13(a). Products, sums and factors each carry signs that the options invert. The product of the roots being negative means the roots have opposite signs — reading the sign as information is faster than checking it as arithmetic
