MUST MASTER · THE THREE HABITS THAT DECIDE THIS CHAPTER

  1. Convert every percentage to a fraction before you multiply. 16⅔% is 1/6, 12½% is 1/8, 37½% is 3/8. A question asking for a 16⅔% increase is asking you to multiply by 7/6 — one step, no decimals
  2. Give the ratio a unit. Never carry 4:3 through a problem as two numbers. Write 4x and 3x, or better, note that one part is worth a definite amount and find that amount first. §2.2
  3. Work in units of work, not fractions of a day. For time and work, take the LCM of the given days as the total work. A job done by A in 12 days and B in 18 becomes 36 units of work at 3 and 2 units a day. No fractions appear anywhere. §6.1

Habit 3 alone will halve your time on §6, and it is the technique most candidates never learn.

1.3 The four standard shapes

MUST MASTER · THE FOUR PERCENTAGE RESULTS WORTH KNOWING BY HEART

1. A is x% more than B, so B is less than A by x/(100 + x) × 100. If A is 25% more than B, then B is 25/125 = 20% less than A. The two percentages are never the same, because the base changes. This is the commonest percentage trap in existence — Q1

2. Increase by x% then decrease by x% is always a net LOSS of x²/100 per cent. Up 20% then down 20% leaves 0.8 × 1.2 = 0.96, a 4% fall — never "no change" — Q8

3. If the price rises by x%, consumption must fall by x/(100 + x) × 100 to keep expenditure the same. A 25% rise needs a 20% cut, not 25% — Q28

4. Two successive discounts of a% and b% equal a single discount of (a + b − ab/100) per cent. 20% and 10% together give 28%, not 30% — Q13

All four are the same underlying point: percentages do not add, because the base moves. Multiply the factors instead — 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72 — and read the answer off the product.

1.4 The base — where most marks are lost

A is 20% of B and B is 20% more than A involve completely different bases. Before computing, name the base in one word: per cent of what? If the question says "increased by", the base is the original value; if it says "of", the base is the noun that follows.

2. Session 2 — Ratio and Proportion

2.1 The basics, stated precisely

A ratio compares quantities of the same kind and has no units. a : b is the fraction a/b, and it is unchanged if both terms are multiplied by the same number — which is why every ratio problem is solved by finding the value of one part.

IdeaStatement
Proportiona : b = c : d, so ad = bc — the product of the extremes equals that of the means
Compounded ratioCombine a:b and b:c by making the common term equal: a:b:c
Duplicate / sub-duplicatea²:b² · and the square-root ratio
Dividing a sumIn a : b : c, each share is its part ÷ total parts of the sum
Direct proportionMore men, more work: multiply
Inverse proportionMore men, fewer days: divide

2.2 The one-part method

MUST MASTER · FIND WHAT ONE PART IS WORTH, THEN ANSWER

Almost every ratio question in this examination reduces to a single line of arithmetic once you find the value of one part.

Worked — a sum divided. ₹6,300 is divided in the ratio 2 : 3 : 4. Total parts = 9, so one part = 700, and the shares are 1,400, 2,100 and 2,800.

Worked — a difference given. A and B share a sum in the ratio 5 : 3, and A gets ₹800 more than B. The difference is 2 parts, so one part = 400, and the total, being 8 parts, is ₹3,200. Note that the question never told you the total — the difference did.

Worked — combining ratios. a : b = 2 : 3 and b : c = 4 : 5. Make b common: multiply the first by 4 and the second by 3 to get a : b : c = 8 : 12 : 15, so a : c = 8 : 15.

The discipline: write down what one part is worth before you look at the options. Candidates who skip that step answer with a part instead of a total, which is exactly the wrong option offered.

2.3 Adding to a ratio — the shape that recurs

When a quantity is added to one component only, the other component is unchanged, and that unchanged quantity is your anchor.

Worked. 45 litres of a mixture is milk and water in the ratio 4 : 1, so milk = 36 L and water = 9 L. How much water makes it 3 : 2? The milk stays 36. In a 3 : 2 mixture with 36 of milk, water must be

  1. So add 15 litres. Q31 is this question.

TRAP · THE UNCHANGED QUANTITY IS THE ONE TO FIX

When water is added, milk is constant. When one salary is raised, the other is constant. Set up the new ratio in terms of the constant, not in terms of a new variable.

Candidates who write "let x litres be added" and solve (36)/(9 + x) = 3/2 arrive at the same answer, but slower and with more chances to slip. Anchoring on the constant makes it mental arithmetic.

  3. Two shares are in the ratio 7 : 4 and differ by ₹900. What is the total?
  4. In 60 litres of a 5 : 1 milk–water mixture, how much water makes it 5 : 2?

3. Session 3 — Averages

3.1 The only formula, and its two rearrangements

Average = total ÷ number of items. Therefore total = average × number, and that second form is the one you actually use.

Almost every average question is solved by converting averages into totals, doing the arithmetic on totals, and converting back. The word "average" in a question is an instruction to multiply.

ShapeMethod
A member is replacedChange in total = number × change in average
A member is addedNew total − old total = the new member's value
Overlapping groupsAdd the group totals and subtract the whole, to isolate the overlap
A wrong reading is correctedChange in average = (error ÷ number of items)

3.2 The replacement rule

MUST MASTER · CHANGE IN TOTAL = NUMBER × CHANGE IN AVERAGE

This one line answers the 2023 "mean of weights" family in a single step.

Worked. The average weight of 8 men is 62 kg. A man weighing 55 kg is replaced and the average becomes 63 kg. The average rose by 1 kg over 8 men, so the total rose by 8 kg, so the new man weighs 55 + 8 = 63 kg.

No equations, no unknowns. The same rule handles a member being added: if 30 members average 32 years and adding one raises the average to 33, the newcomer's age is 31 × 33 − 30 × 32 = 63 — Q16.

And corrections: if one observation of 96 was read as 69, the total is short by 27, so over 25 observations the average is short by 27/25 = 1.08 — Q30.

3.3 Overlapping groups — the subtraction trick

Worked. A, B and C average 45 kg. A and B average 40; B and C average 43. Find B.

(A + B) + (B + C) = 80 + 86 = 166, and A + B + C = 135. Subtracting, B = 31 kg.

The pattern generalises: where two overlapping pairs and the whole are given, the sum of the pairs minus the whole leaves the overlap. Q25 and Q10 are both this shape.

4. Session 4 — Mixtures and Alligation

4.1 The alligation rule

For two ingredients mixed to a known mean value:

(quantity of cheaper) : (quantity of dearer) = (price of dearer − mean) : (mean − price of cheaper)

The differences are taken crosswise, and the result is a ratio of quantities.

Worked. Milk at ₹40 a litre is mixed with water, which is free, so that the mixture costs ₹32 a litre. Ratio of milk to water = (32 − 0) : (40 − 32) = 32 : 8 = 4 : 1.

Worked, with two mixtures. Vessel I has milk and water in the ratio 5 : 2, vessel II in the ratio 3 : 4. In what ratio must they be mixed to get equal milk and water? Work in milk fractions: 5/7 and 3/7, target 1/2. Ratio = (1/2 − 3/7) : (5/7 − 1/2) = 1/14 : 3/14 = 1 : 3.

MUST MASTER · ALWAYS ALLIGATE ON THE SAME COMPONENT

The single mechanical error in alligation is mixing the components: taking the milk fraction of one vessel and the water fraction of the other.

Choose one component — milk, or alcohol, or the cheaper item — and express all three values as that component's share. Then apply the crosswise rule.

And note what the answer is: a ratio of the quantities of the two mixtures, not of their components. Q27 offers the reversed ratio 3 : 1 as an option for exactly this reason.

4.2 Repeated replacement

When y units are drawn from x units of pure liquid and replaced by water, and this is done n times:

Liquid remaining = x × [(x − y)/x]ⁿ

Worked. 20 litres of milk; 4 litres drawn off and replaced by water, twice. Milk left = 20 × (16/20)² = 20 × 0.64 = 12.8 litres — Q19.

Note the trap: the formula gives the liquid remaining, not the amount removed, and it applies only when the volume is restored each time.

5. Session 5 — Problems on Ages

5.1 The method

Ages are ratio problems with a time shift. Three rules make them mechanical:

  1. Let the present ages be the ratio with an x — 7x and 3x, not two separate unknowns. One variable, one equation
  2. Add or subtract the shift from EVERY age. Ten years hence, both are ten years older
  3. Form the equation from the second ratio and cross-multiply

Worked — the 2023 ratio-and-ages shape. The ages of a father and son are in the ratio 7 : 3, and ten years hence the ratio will be 2 : 1. Then (7x + 10)/(3x + 10) = 2/1, so 7x + 10 = 6x + 20, giving x = 10 and the father's present age 70. Check: 70 and 30 become 80 and 40, which is 2 : 1 — Q26.

MUST MASTER · CHECK BY SUBSTITUTION, IT COSTS FIVE SECONDS

Age problems are the easiest place in the Quant paper to make a sign error, and the only place where verification is completely free: put your answer back into the words of the question.

If the father is 70 and the son 30, then in ten years they are 80 and 40 — a ratio of 2 : 1, as required. Five seconds, and it converts a probable mark into a certain one.

The examiner's standard trap is to offer the value of x, or the son's age, when the father's was asked. In the worked example, x = 10 and the son is 30; both are wrong answers to the question asked. Before you mark, re-read whose age was wanted.

5.2 The two other standard forms

FormExampleEquation
A multiple now, another laterA father is 3 times his son's age; in 12 years, twice3s + 12 = 2(s + 12), so s = 12
A multiple now, another earlier3 times now; 15 years ago, 9 times3s − 15 = 9(s − 15), so s = 20

Both are one linear equation. Note in the second that the larger multiple belongs to the earlier time — ratios of ages always move towards 1 : 1 as people get older, which is a useful sanity check on any answer.

6. Session 6 — Time and Work

6.1 The LCM method — the technique to learn

MUST MASTER · TAKE THE TOTAL WORK AS THE LCM OF THE DAYS

Do not add fractions. Take the total work as the LCM of the given numbers of days, and every rate becomes a whole number of units per day.

Worked. A does a job in 12 days, B in 18. LCM = 36 units of work. A does 3 units a day, B does 2. Together 5 a day, so they finish in 36/5 = 7.2 days — Q7.

Worked, one leaves. A in 20 days, B in 30. LCM = 60 units; A does 3 a day, B does 2, together 5. In 6 days they complete 30 units — exactly half. The remaining 30 units at A's 3 a day take 10 days — Q29.

Worked, efficiency given. A is twice as efficient as B and together they take 12 days. Let B do 1 unit a day and A 2, so together 3 a day and the job is 36 units. B alone: 36 days — Q21.

Every fraction has disappeared. This is the single highest-return technique in the chapter, and it also prevents the commonest error in the topic — adding days instead of adding rates.

6.2 The rules

SituationRule
TogetherAdd the rates, never the days
A alone, from togetherB's rate = (together's rate) − (A's rate)
EfficiencyTwice as efficient means half the time; rates are in the ratio of efficiencies
WagesWages divide in the ratio of work done, not of days worked
Men and daysM₁D₁/W₁ = M₂D₂/W₂ — more men, fewer days

6.3 Wages — the shape that catches people

Worked. A and B contract a job for ₹2,400. A alone would take 20 days, B 30. With C's help they finish in 10 days. What is C's share?

Take 60 units. A does 3 a day, B 2, so A + B = 5. To finish in 10 days the team must do 6 a day, so C does 1 unit a day. Over 10 days: A 30 units, B 20, C 10 — a sixth of the work, so ₹400 — Q35.

The trap is to divide the money by three, or in the ratio of days. Wages follow work done.

7. Session 7 — The Lean Tail

Three topics that are not in the 2023 evidence but are one step from what is. They get one session between them, and that is a deliberate judgement about yield, not an oversight: percentage is the parent of all three, so if you have §1 you have most of this already.

7.1 Average speed

Average speed = total distance ÷ total time. Never the average of the speeds.

For equal distances at speeds u and v, average speed = 2uv/(u + v) — the harmonic mean.

Worked. Half the distance at 40 km/h, half at 60. Average = 2 × 40 × 60/100 = 48 km/h, not 50 — Q23. It is below 50 because more time is spent at the slower speed.

For equal times, the average is the arithmetic mean. Equal distances give the harmonic mean; equal times give the arithmetic mean — and the question always tells you which by saying half the distance or half the time. F9.1 §2.2's conversion applies here: km/h to m/s is × 5/18.

7.2 Profit and loss

QuantityFormula
Profit %(SP − CP)/CP × 100 — always on cost
SP from a gainSP = CP × (100 + gain%)/100
CP from SPCP = SP × 100/(100 + gain%)
DiscountOn the marked price, not on cost

TRAP · EQUAL SELLING PRICES, EQUAL PERCENTAGES, ONE GAIN ONE LOSS » ALWAYS A LOSS

Two articles sold at the same price, one at a gain of x% and one at a loss of x%, always produce an overall loss of x²/100 per cent.

Worked. Two articles at ₹1,200 each, one at +20%, one at −20%. Cost prices are 1,200 × 100/120 = 1,000 and 1,200 × 100/80 = 1,500, so total cost 2,500 against total sale 2,400: a loss of ₹100 on ₹2,500 = 4% — which is 20²/100 — Q33.

The reason is the same as §1.3's second result: the two percentages have different bases. The loss is taken on the larger cost price, so it outweighs the gain. "No profit no loss" is the wrong option waiting for you.

7.3 Simple and compound interest

SI = PRT/100. CI: amount = P(1 + R/100)ᵗ, and CI = amount − P.

ResultValue
Difference between CI and SI, 2 yearsP(R/100)²
Difference, 3 yearsP(R/100)² × (300 + R)/100
Compounded half-yearlyRate halves, time doubles
Money doubles under SIWhen RT = 100

Worked. ₹10,000 for 2 years at 10%. SI = 2,000. CI = 10,000 × 1.21 − 10,000 = 2,100. Difference ₹100, which is 10,000 × (10/100)² — Q32.

The two-year difference is worth memorising, because it converts a two-line computation into a one-step multiplication, and it is the only form of the question that is commonly set.

THINK LIKE UPSC · THE FIVE SHAPES IN THIS BANK

  1. The moving base. Q1, Q8, Q13, Q28, Q33. Five of thirty-five questions are the same insight — percentages cannot be added or reversed, because the base changes. Multiply the factors instead. Learning this one idea properly is worth more than any other page in the chapter
  2. The wrong quantity, correctly computed. Q3 offers 2 parts and 3 parts; Q5 offers B's age; Q16 offers the new average; Q26 offers the son's age; Q31 offers the new total water. In every case the arithmetic is right and the question was different. Before marking, re-read what was asked
  3. Averages as totals. Q4, Q10, Q16, Q25, Q30. Never work with averages directly — multiply up to totals, do the arithmetic, divide back
  4. LCM instead of fractions. Q7, Q15, Q21, Q29, Q35. Five questions, no fractions needed in any of them
  5. The intuitive answer offered first. Q8(a) "no change", Q13(b) "30%", Q23(a) "50", Q28(b) "25%", Q33(a) "neither profit nor loss". In each case option (a) or (b) is what a candidate answers without calculating. Where an option looks like the obvious symmetry, that is the reason to compute
